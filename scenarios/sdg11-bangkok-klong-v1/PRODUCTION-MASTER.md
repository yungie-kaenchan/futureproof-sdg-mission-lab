## 1.3 The empirical spine of the tension

This scenario is built on a **documented structural conflict**, not an invented one:

- Bangkok is a **subsiding delta city** whose flood risk is rising over time while a large, long-settled population lives **inside the canal infrastructure itself**, frequently **without secure land title**. (Widely documented in Thai and international urban-studies and disaster literature; held by BMA / NHA / CODI / academic monitoring.)
- Hard-engineered flood corridors (widening, floodwalls, polder + pumping) **demonstrably reduce flood losses for the protected catchment** — *and* require the **canal reservation to be cleared**, which **displaces informal-tenure households** who have the **fewest legal protections and the least capacity to absorb a forced move**.
- A **consented, funded relocation-and-tenure pathway exists in principle** — the **Baan Mankong / CODI** model has resettled and secured tenure for canal-side communities elsewhere in Bangkok — **but it is slower than an engineering timeline and is not automatically funded for a given corridor**. The gap between *what protects the city fastest* and *what treats the displaced justly* is therefore **real, structural, and current**, not fabricated.
- **Procedural justice** is the live second axis: who is *consulted*, *when*, and *with what power to change the alignment*, determines whether a technically sound corridor is also legitimate — and unconsulted infrastructure in informal-tenure areas has a documented record of resistance, delay, and worsened outcomes.

> The scenario does **not** assert that any specific Bangkok canal community has been displaced by any specific named project. It composes a **plausible decision case** from the structural pattern above.

### 1.3.1 The two ethical axes, made concrete

So the pedagogical machinery has a precise target, each axis is anchored to a *specific* contested claim in the dossier, not a vague value:

- **Urban resilience vs. housing justice** becomes concrete at the moment the corridor's *non-hydraulic* cost is named (Dossier Part 3): the catchment's flood-loss reduction is **collective and quantifiable**, while the displacement cost is **concentrated, legally unprotected, and partly invisible to the corridor's own budget**. The axis is not "city vs. poor" in the abstract — it is *whose loss appears on the ledger and whose does not*.
- **Procedural justice (who is consulted)** becomes concrete at the sequencing fork (Dossier Part 4, Source B, Stakeholder 4): the same physical works can be **legitimate or illegitimate depending solely on whether consent and a funded settlement precede or follow the fixed alignment**. Procedural justice here is *instrumental as well as ethical* — the dossier's strongest empirical claim is that consent-last produces both worse terms *and* longer delays, which is what makes "fast vs. fair" a false binary the strongest learners dismantle.

These two anchors are what the DECIDE cross-examiner Rounds 2 and 3 (§2.9) and the PROBE source-pair gate (§2.7) are calibrated against.

## 1.6 Cultural-authenticity guard (CLAUDE.md §11 + §15; Reconstruction Master §2 dignity guardrail)

Klong-side life is handled with **dignity, specificity, and agency — never poverty-porn**:

- The **klong-side resident** is a **rights-bearing, knowledgeable city-dweller**, not a victim-prop. Her voice is grounded, practical, and clear-eyed about the flood risk — she is not "anti-flood-protection," she is anti-*being-erased-by-it*. She speaks of the canal as **home and as drainage** in the same breath, because that is the lived truth. No squalor imagery, no helplessness framing; she has tenure *insecurity*, not absence of standing.
- The **BMA flood-engineering official** speaks in measured technocratic English of the kind heard in Thai municipal briefings — responsible, public-service register, **not villainous**. Her duty to the catchment is genuine.
- The **CBD business / property interest** speaks polished commercial English (investor-deck register) — legitimate economic concern, **not a cartoon developer**.
- The **community-network organiser / urban-studies bridging voice** speaks warmly but rigorously, citing the **Baan Mankong precedent** as lived practice, not theory — modelling how Thai community-network organisers actually talk: collective, procedural, dignified.
- **Informal tenure is explained as a structural-legal fact, not a moral failing.** Living over the water for generations is rendered as *heritage and adaptation*, not deficiency. The Baan Mankong precedent is presented as evidence that a *just* path is operationally real, so the scenario does not collapse into fatalism.

**No stakeholder is positioned as "the wrong answer." All four positions are defensible from inside the speaker's situation.**

### 1.6.1 Local Lens depth (mandatory layer)

The Local Lens is not a translation veneer — it is the layer that makes this an *authentically Thai* urban dilemma rather than a generic "flood vs. slum" template:

- **Klong heritage framing.** The dossier names the canal network as "the city's old transport grid ('Venice of the East')" so the klong reads as **civic heritage and adaptation**, not as a backwater. Living over the water is rendered as an inherited urban form, which is the Thai social reality.
- **Tenure as a Thai legal-historical fact.** "Informal tenure" is glossed (EN + Thai: การถือครองแบบไม่เป็นทางการ / ไม่มีเอกสารสิทธิ์) and explained structurally — built where parents built, no โฉนด — so a Thai learner recognises the actual land-administration situation and a non-Thai learner is not invited to read it as moral failure.
- **The Baan Mankong precedent is the Local-Lens spine.** บ้านมั่นคง / CODI is a *Thai-originated, internationally cited* community-driven model. Foregrounding it (Source B, Stakeholder 4, Dossier Part 4) is what prevents the scenario from collapsing into imported fatalism: the just path is Thai, proven, and named in Thai.
- **Thai institutional realism.** BMA, the Drainage & Sewerage department, RID, NHA, CODI, DDPM, the Ministry of Interior frame — these are the *actual* bodies a Thai planner would convene, so the coalition the resolution requires is institutionally literate, not invented.
- **Register authenticity.** Stakeholder English registers match how these Thai roles actually speak English in municipal/civic settings (municipal-briefing technocratic, community-meeting plain-firm, investor-relations polished, community-organiser collective-procedural) — see §1.6.

## 1.7 Flood-history grounding (why the hazard is credible, not invented)

The scenario does not depend on a single dramatic event; it rests on a **recurring, structurally rising** hazard pattern that any Thai reader will recognise:

- Bangkok has a **documented history of severe basin-scale flooding**, the most widely remembered being the **2011 Chao Phraya floods**, which inundated large parts of the central region for an extended period and produced economic losses the country absorbed for years. (Magnitude held by RID / DDPM / national economic agencies — *cited here as a recognised event, not quantified to a fabricated figure*.)
- Beyond the headline events, the city experiences **routine seasonal inundation** of low-lying districts during the monsoon-plus-high-tide overlap, managed (often barely) by the klong-pump-floodgate system. This is the *ordinary* condition the corridor is designed against — which is precisely why the BMA engineer's anxiety is legitimate and not alarmist.
- The hazard is **non-stationary**: subsidence plus a heavier rainfall regime plus sea-level rise mean the design problem **worsens over the planning horizon**. This is what gives the scenario its intergenerational edge — a "wait and see" answer is not neutral; the exposure grows while the decision is deferred.

This grounding is what keeps **Source A from being a strawman**: the flood risk it cites is real, the delay cost is genuine, and a strong learner must hold the protection warrant *and* the justice critique simultaneously.

## 1.8 What makes this a genuine dilemma (designer's note)

A weak version of this scenario would be "ruthless city vs. helpless poor" — which the dignity guard (§1.6) and the anti-single-hero check (§1.4) both forbid. The dilemma is genuine because **all of the following are simultaneously true**:

1. The corridor **does** protect ~80,000 commuters and the CBD, and the flood loss it averts is large and falls partly on ordinary workers, not only on property.
2. The displaced households **do** hold the weakest legal position and the lowest capacity to absorb a forced move — and "standard procedure" has a documented record of scattering communities and exporting hidden public cost.
3. A just pathway (**Baan Mankong / CODI**) is **operationally real**, not utopian — so fatalism ("nothing better is possible") is itself a defeasible claim a strong learner should reject.
4. **Sequencing**, not the existence of consent, is the true cost driver: consent-last has a documented record of producing *both* worse settlement terms *and* longer delays — meaning the "fast" engineering path may not actually be faster once resistance is priced in.

The strongest learner positions are therefore **conditional and sequenced**, never flat. This note governs how the DECIDE-stage AI cross-examiner and the PROBE source-pair applier calibrate Band 4+ vs Band 2 (see §2.7 source-pair note and §3.5 calibration anchors).

# 2. SCENARIO TEXT

## 2.1 Title

**The Klong and the City**
*Thai:* **คลองกับเมือง**

## 2.2 Setting (28 words)

> Bangkok is a sinking delta. Its old canals — its klongs — drain the monsoon away from millions. Families have lived on and over that same water for generations.

## 2.3 Core tension (22 words)

> A flood-defence corridor would shield 80,000 commuters and the business district — but erase klong-side homes whose families hold no land title.

## 2.4 Decision point (framed for the DECIDE stage)

> Should the city proceed with the engineered flood-defence corridor on its **current alignment and timeline** — *before* a funded, consented relocation-and-tenure settlement exists for the affected klong households?

## 2.5 Ethical axes (controlled vocabulary)

1. **Urban resilience vs. housing justice** — the protected catchment (commuters, the central business district, the wider city's flood-loss reduction) gains; the canal-reservation households (informal tenure, generational residence, low capacity to absorb a forced move) bear the cost. Who is protected, and at whose expense?
2. **Procedural justice (who is consulted)** — is the alignment fixed *before* the affected community has real power to change it, or is consent and a funded settlement a *precondition* of the works? A technically sound corridor can still be illegitimate if it is decided over the people inside it.

### DOSSIER PART 1 — "A city built on water"

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> Bangkok is built on very flat, low land near the sea. In some places the ground is only a metre or two above the water. The city is also slowly sinking. So when heavy rain, river water, and the sea all come together, water has nowhere to go.
>
> To stay dry, the city uses old canals. A canal is called a <vocab>klong</vocab>. The klongs carry rain and flood water away to the river and the sea. Gates and big pumps push the water out. This whole system is the city's flood <vocab>defence</vocab>.
>
> But the klongs are not empty. For many years — for whole families, for generations — people have lived right on the water. Their houses stand on wooden posts over the canal. The klong is a machine that protects the city. It is also somebody's home.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2, baseline):**

> Bangkok sits on the flat, low delta of the Chao Phraya River, close to the Gulf of Thailand. In places the built-up land lies only a metre or two above the sea, and the delta is still slowly <vocab>subsiding</vocab>. When monsoon rain, upstream river discharge, and a high tide arrive together, the water has very little room to drain away.
>
> To stay dry, the city depends on a centuries-old network of canals. A canal here is a <vocab>klong</vocab>. The klongs move stormwater toward the river and the Gulf, helped by floodgates and large pumping stations. Together, this network is the city's flood <vocab>defence</vocab> infrastructure.
>
> But the klongs are not just infrastructure. For generations, families have lived *on and over* the water — stilt houses, walkways, and platforms built along and above the canal. The same klong is, at once, a drainage machine for millions and a long-settled <vocab>community</vocab>'s home.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> Bangkok occupies the low, flat alluvial delta of the lower Chao Phraya, immediately inland of the Gulf of Thailand; in places the urbanised surface stands only a metre or two above mean sea level, and the delta continues to <vocab>subside</vocab> incrementally. When monsoon rainfall, upstream discharge, and tidal backflow coincide, the system's capacity to evacuate water is severely constrained.
>
> The city's resilience rests on a centuries-old <vocab>klong</vocab> network — canals that, with floodgates and high-capacity pumping stations, route stormwater toward the river and the Gulf. This network constitutes the metropolitan flood-<vocab>defence</vocab> infrastructure.
>
> Yet the klong is not merely infrastructure. Over generations, households have settled *on and above* the water itself — stilted dwellings, walkways and platforms threaded along the canal reservation. The same channel is simultaneously a hydraulic asset serving millions and the established home of a long-resident <vocab>community</vocab>.

### DOSSIER PART 2 — "Why a corridor, and who it protects"

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> One big klong has become too small. It cannot carry away the water from the worst storms. When it floods, the water reaches a busy part of the city — the central business <vocab>district</vocab>, where many offices are, and the roads that around 80,000 people use to get to work each day.
>
> The city has a plan. It will make the klong wider, build a strong wall along it, and add bigger pumps. This is the flood-defence <vocab>corridor</vocab>. The plan is allowed by law. The city owns the strip of land next to the canal — the canal <vocab>reservation</vocab>.
>
> If the corridor is built, the business district and the commuters are much safer in a big flood. That is a real and good thing. The question is what the plan costs, and who pays it.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2, baseline):**

> One major klong can no longer carry the volume the largest storms now produce. When it overtops, the flooding reaches a busy zone of the city — the central business <vocab>district</vocab>, with its offices, and the commuter routes used by **roughly 80,000 people** every working day.
>
> The Bangkok Metropolitan Administration has approved a plan: widen the canal, build a continuous floodwall along it, and upgrade the pumping-and-drainage capacity. This is the flood-defence <vocab>corridor</vocab>. The plan is lawful, and the city holds the strip of land beside the canal — the canal <vocab>reservation</vocab>.
>
> If the corridor is built on its current line, the business district and those commuters are far better protected in a major flood. That benefit is real and serious. The contested questions are what the plan costs in *other* terms — and who is made to pay them.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> One principal klong can no longer convey the discharge that contemporary extreme storms generate; when it is overtopped, the resulting inundation reaches a high-value zone — the central business <vocab>district</vocab> and the commuter corridors carrying **on the order of 80,000 daily travellers**.
>
> The Bangkok Metropolitan Administration has authorised an engineered response: canal widening, a continuous floodwall, and a pumping-and-drainage upgrade — collectively, the flood-defence <vocab>corridor</vocab>. The measure is lawful, and the municipality holds the canal <vocab>reservation</vocab>, the statutory land strip flanking the channel.
>
> Delivered on its current alignment, the corridor materially reduces flood exposure for the business district and those commuters — a substantial and defensible public benefit. What remains contested is the corridor's *non-hydraulic* cost, and the distribution of who is required to bear it.

### DOSSIER PART 3 — "Who lives in the way, and why title matters"

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> To make the klong wider and build the wall, the houses on the canal <vocab>reservation</vocab> must be cleared or moved back. On the affected part of the canal, this means somewhere between several hundred and a few thousand homes. The families there have lived on the water for generations.
>
> Most of them do not have a land <vocab>title</vocab> — a legal paper that says the land is theirs. They built where their parents and grandparents built. This is called <vocab>informal</vocab> tenure: real homes, real community, but no secure legal right to stay.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2, baseline):**

> To widen the klong and build the floodwall, the structures on the canal <vocab>reservation</vocab> must be cleared or set back. Along the affected reaches this involves an estimated **several hundred to a few thousand households**, many of whom have lived over the water for generations.
>
> Most hold no land <vocab>title</vocab> — no secure legal document of ownership. They built where their families built before them. This is <vocab>informal</vocab> tenure: an established home and community, but without a protected legal right to remain.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> Realising the widening and the floodwall requires that the structures occupying the canal <vocab>reservation</vocab> be cleared or set back; along the affected reaches this implicates an estimated **several hundred to a few thousand households**, many with generational tenure on the water.
>
> The majority hold no land <vocab>title</vocab> — no secure instrument of ownership — having built within an inherited occupation pattern. This is <vocab>informal</vocab> tenure: a materially established community lacking a legally protected right of continued residence.
>
> Absent title, displacement can proceed with constrained compensation and minimal procedural voice, dissolving dwelling, social network, and a centrally located livelihood foothold concurrently. These households possess the least adaptive capacity to absorb forced relocation; the corridor, on its present alignment, concentrates its distributive burden precisely upon them.

### DOSSIER PART 4 — "The decision before you"

### SOURCE B — University urban-studies / community-network summary (224 words)

### Source-pair note for the AI applier (PROBE scoring)

When scoring PROBE responses, the AI applier should treat **Source B as the more defensible** on three grounds — **provenance** (independent academic + community-network vs. the delivering programme's own engineering team, which has a delivery interest), **method** (Source B engages comparable Bangkok displacement *outcomes* and a *named, evidenced precedent*; Source A rests on hydraulic modelling plus an unexamined "standard procedure"), and **disclosed interest** (Source A's recommendation aligns with its author's mandate to deliver on schedule; Source B discloses its partnership and still concedes the flood benefit). ## 2.9 DECIDE stage — tribunal / decision frame

> The city flood committee convenes a closed session. The team must take and defend a position on the decision question:
>
> *Should the city proceed with the engineered flood-defence corridor on its current alignment and timeline, before a funded, consented relocation-and-tenure settlement exists for the affected klong households?*
>
> This is a genuine dilemma — there is no costless answer. The strongest positions are **conditional and sequenced** (e.g. "proceed with the non-displacing works in parallel *while* making a funded, co-designed settlement a binding precondition for any clearance"), not flat yes/no.
>
> The AI cross-examiner probes across **four rounds**, intensity calibrated to response quality (DECIDE stage allows **one retry** before the score locks, per Reconstruction Master §3.1):
>
> 1. **Round 1 — premise check.** "You have argued X. What is the evidence X rests on — the flood model, the displacement count range, or the Baan Mankong precedent?"
> 2. **Round 2 — stakeholder challenge.** "The BMA engineer says every delayed monsoon is an indefensible flood exposure to 80,000 commuters. If you sequence consent first, how do you answer *her* strongest reason?"
> 3. **Round 3 — procedural / temporal challenge.** "You are deciding the future of households who hold no title and may not be in this room. By what authority — and through what *process* — do you bind them?"
> 4. **Round 4 — concession invitation.** "Is there one premise in your position you would now revise, with reasoning?"
>
> **Graceful Concession bonus:** a team that revises one premise *with reasoning* in Round 4 earns the bonus; a team that collapses to "it depends" earns zero. (Mirrors the Khon Kaen tribunal economy; exact token values inherit the engine default.)
>
> **DECIDE output:** a 180–260 word position statement that becomes the spine of the team's ACT-stage artifact and carries forward as evidence into the Final Task (§3.4).
