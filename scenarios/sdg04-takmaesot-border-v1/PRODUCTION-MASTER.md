## 1.3 The empirical spine of the tension

This scenario is built on a **documented**, not invented, conflict. The tension does not rely on a single statistic; it rests on a structural fact pattern that the literature consistently describes:

> **The recurring finding in Thai border-education research and UN access reviews:** Thailand's Education for All policy gives every child — Thai, migrant, stateless, undocumented — a *legal right* to enrol in a government school. In the Tak border context that right is **real but not self-executing**: the language of instruction is Thai; teacher posts and classrooms are finite; many families cannot absorb the indirect costs; and the *value of the credential after school* depends on legal status. Migrant Learning Centres grew up precisely because the formal system could not, on its own, absorb the demand — but MLCs run mostly on **external funding** and offer schooling whose **onward recognition is uncertain**.

The genuine dilemma, therefore, is **not** "should children be educated" (everyone in the scenario agrees they should). It is an **allocation-under-scarcity** dilemma with a **recognition** dimension:

- A district has a fixed envelope of teacher posts, classroom capacity, and bridging budget.
- **Mainstreaming** migrant and stateless children into Thai government schools delivers a *recognised* pathway and full legal-right fulfilment — but only works well if Thai-language bridging, teacher supply, transport and credential-recognition support are funded *first*.
- **Sustaining MLCs** keeps children learning *now*, in a language they understand, close to home — but on donor-dependent money and with weaker onward recognition.
- Doing the first by **redirecting money away from the second, before the bridging plan is funded**, risks a gap in which children fall *out* of both systems. That gap — not a number — is the harm the decision must reason about.

Illustrative magnitudes, where they appear in student-facing text, are given as **clearly-labelled ranges or qualifiers** ("a substantial share," "in the thousands across the border area," "reports describe a large but uncounted population"), never as invented precise figures. See §1.5.

## 1.5 Flagged uncertainties — use ranges, never invent

The following are knowingly **not** specified to a precise figure in any student-facing text. Where they appear, the dossier and sources use qualifiers ("a large but uncounted population," "reports describe," "a substantial share," "in the thousands across the border area"):

- ⚠ Exact number of stateless and migrant children of school age in the Tak border area — there is **no single authoritative live figure**; the population is large, mobile, and partly uncounted. Dossier says *"a large, partly uncounted population — reliable estimates run into the thousands across the border area."*
- ⚠ Exact number of Migrant Learning Centres operating in the district at any moment — fluctuates with funding and displacement. Dossier uses *"dozens of community learning centres of varying size."*
- ⚠ Exact share of border migrant/stateless children enrolled in government schools vs MLCs vs out of school — not a stable single number. Dossier uses *"a meaningful share are in Thai government schools; many more are in community learning centres; a number are in neither."*
- ⚠ The "accelerated timeline" the district proposes — fictionalized as **one academic year** for narrative tractability. Flagged here as a *narrative choice, not a verified plan*. Acceptable because the scenario is a *plausible decision case*, not a documentary.
- ⚠ Exact MLC funding amounts or the size of the district bridging budget — deliberately not used; framed as "the district's bridging envelope" and "donor funding the centres depend on."
- ⚠ Specific NGO names, specific school names, specific official names — deliberately not used.

## 1.6 Cultural-authenticity & dignity guard

This is the section that, more than any other, determines whether the scenario is fit to ship.

**Representing stateless and migrant children and their families:**

- Children and parents are **agents with judgement about their own lives**, never problem-objects to be solved by others. The parent stakeholder reasons about trade-offs, costs, and her child's future — she is not a recipient of rescue.
- **No poverty-porn.** No language or imagery of squalor, helplessness, dirt, or pity. Hardship is named plainly and only where it bears on the decision (cost, language, credentials), never aestheticised.
- **No saviourism.** No stakeholder — NGO, official, academic — is positioned as the hero who fixes it. The resolution the learners must reach is a *coalition + sequencing* outcome, structurally incapable of being a single-hero answer.
- **Statelessness handled accurately and without stigma.** "Stateless" describes a *legal condition*, not a deficiency in the child. The text consistently couples it with the child's right and capability, never with blame.
- **The Myanmar side is referenced factually and minimally** — as the reason families are present (border, displacement, migration) — without narrating, taking a position on, or dramatising events across the border. No armed-conflict scene-setting.
- **Multiple legitimate Thai perspectives, fairly.** The District Education officer who must ration scarce posts is **not a villain**; her constraint is real and the scenario lets her state it with dignity. The principal worried about capacity is **not callous**; his concern about doing right by *all* children in an over-subscribed school is legitimate. Mainstreaming and MLC-sustaining are both defensible positions held by reasonable people.

**Stakeholder voice register (per CLAUDE.md §11 + §15):**

- The MLC teacher speaks with grounded classroom detail, warmth, and professional pride — **not** as a struggling volunteer to be pitied. She is an educator with expertise.
- The parent of a stateless child speaks carefully, practically, and with quiet authority about her child's future — hedged, plain, dignified; **not** a folksy or tearful caricature.
- The District Education officer speaks in measured Thai-administrative English — responsible, constrained, fair-minded; the register of a public servant under genuine resource pressure.
- The Thai government-school principal speaks plainly and with institutional honesty about capacity, fairness to *all* his pupils, and what he can and cannot promise.
- The NGO education coordinator speaks in calm, evidence-anchored bridging English — naming the sequencing risk without grandstanding.

**No stakeholder is positioned as "the wrong answer." All four positions are defensible from inside the speaker's situation. The dilemma is real precisely because decent people disagree.**

> Mae Sot sits on the Moei River, the Thai–Myanmar border in Tak Province. For years, migrant and stateless children here have learned in two parallel systems at once.

> Every child has a legal right to school in Thailand — but at the border, status, language, funding and credentials decide whether that right works.

> Should the district mainstream migrant and stateless children into Thai government schools on an accelerated, one-year timeline — redirecting funding away from community Migrant Learning Centres — *before* a Thai-language-bridging, teacher-supply, and credential-recognition plan is funded and in place?

## 2.5 Ethical axes (controlled vocabulary)

1. **Right to education vs. resource scarcity** — Thailand's Education for All policy makes the right unconditional; a district's teacher posts, classrooms and bridging budget are not. Who absorbs the cost of fulfilling the right *now* vs. fulfilling it *well*?
2. **Belonging & recognition (status, credentials, language)** — a seat in a Thai classroom is also a question of language a child can learn in, a credential the system will recognise afterward, and whether the child is counted as belonging. The decision allocates not only money but recognition.

### Part 2 — Why the right is hard to deliver

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> The rule says every child can join a Thai school. But four problems make this hard at the border.
>
> First, language. Thai schools teach in Thai. Many migrant children speak Burmese, Karen or Mon. Without Thai-language <vocab>bridging</vocab> help, they fall behind fast.
>
> Second, not enough teachers and seats. A school has a <vocab>capacity</vocab> — the most children it can teach well. Border schools are already full. There are not enough teacher posts.
>
> Third, cost for families. School itself may be free, but uniforms, transport and time are not. Poor families feel this.
>
> Fourth, the certificate at the end. School should give a child a <vocab>credential</vocab> — a record of what they learned. For a <vocab>stateless</vocab> child, it is not always clear that this record will be accepted later. Thailand does give non-Thai children a special student ID number so they can be in the system and get records. But families still worry about the future.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2, baseline):**

> The right exists; delivering it at the border runs into four real frictions.
>
> *Language.* Thai government schools teach in Thai. Many migrant and stateless children's first languages are Burmese, Karen or Mon. Without funded Thai-language <vocab>bridging</vocab> support, a child placed straight into a Thai classroom can fall behind quickly and disengage.
>
> *Teacher supply and capacity.* A school's <vocab>capacity</vocab> is the number of pupils it can teach properly. Border-area government schools are often already at or over <vocab>capacity</vocab>, and teacher posts are finite. Adding a large new cohort without new posts stretches every classroom — including those of children already enrolled.
>
> *Household cost.* Even where tuition is free, families face indirect costs: transport, materials, lost income. For low-income migrant households this is a real barrier to sustained <vocab>enrolment</vocab>.
>
> *Credential recognition.* A child should leave school with a <vocab>credential</vocab> the system will honour. Thailand assigns non-Thai children a 13-digit student identifier so they can be entered into the system and receive education records — an important mechanism. But for a <vocab>stateless</vocab> child, the onward <vocab>recognition</vocab> of that record is less certain than for a Thai citizen, and families weigh that uncertainty.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> The entitlement is unconditional in law; its delivery at the border is constrained by four interacting frictions.
>
> *Medium of instruction.* Government schooling proceeds in Thai, whereas the first languages of much of the cohort are Burmese, Karen or Mon. Absent funded Thai-language <vocab>bridging</vocab> provision, direct placement tends to produce rapid academic disengagement rather than inclusion.
>
> *Teacher supply and capacity.* School <vocab>capacity</vocab> — the throughput at which instruction remains adequate — is frequently already saturated in border-area government schools, and establishment posts are fixed. Absorbing a large additional cohort without commensurate posts dilutes provision for the incoming and incumbent pupils alike.
>
> *Indirect household cost.* Nominal tuition-free status does not eliminate transport, materials and opportunity costs, which constitute a material barrier to sustained <vocab>enrolment</vocab> for low-income migrant households.
>
> *Credential recognition.* Schooling should terminate in a <vocab>credential</vocab> the system will honour. Thailand's allocation of a thirteen-digit non-Thai student identifier enables system entry and record-keeping — a substantive mechanism — yet for a <vocab>stateless</vocab> child the onward <vocab>recognition</vocab> of that record remains less assured than for a citizen, and households rationally price that residual uncertainty into their schooling choices.

### Part 3 — Why the Migrant Learning Centres exist

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> The Migrant Learning Centres did not appear by accident. They grew because the government schools could not take every child at once. The centres teach in languages the children already know. They are close to home. Children can start learning today, not next year.
>
> But the centres have a weak point. Their money mostly comes from outside donors, not from the regular Thai school budget. If donors stop, a centre can close. And the learning record from a centre is not always accepted later as a full Thai school <vocab>credential</vocab>. So the centres keep children learning now — but their future path is less certain.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2, baseline):**

> The Migrant Learning Centres were not a policy design; they emerged because the formal system could not, on its own, absorb the demand. Their strengths are real: instruction in a language the child already understands, proximity to home, and immediate access — a child can be learning *this* term rather than waiting for a place and a bridging plan that may not yet be funded.
>
> Their vulnerability is equally real. The centres run largely on **external, donor-dependent funding**, not the regular Thai per-pupil budget line, so they are exposed to funding shifts. And learning completed at a centre does not always convert cleanly into a recognised Thai-system <vocab>credential</vocab>. The centres therefore secure learning *now* while leaving the *onward pathway* less certain — the mirror image of the government-school trade-off.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> The Migrant Learning Centres are not an artefact of policy design but an endogenous response to a formal system that could not unilaterally absorb demand. Their comparative advantages are substantive: instruction in the learner's first language, spatial proximity, and immediacy of access — enrolment in the current term rather than deferral pending a place and a bridging plan that may itself be unfunded.
>
> Their structural exposure is correspondingly substantive. Provision is sustained largely by **external, donor-contingent funding** rather than the recurrent per-pupil allocation, rendering it sensitive to funding reallocation; and attainment within a centre does not reliably convert into a recognised formal-system <vocab>credential</vocab>. The centres thus secure learning in the present while leaving the onward pathway comparatively under-secured — the precise inverse of the government-school trade-off, which secures pathway recognition while under-securing immediate, comprehensible access.

## 2.7 Conflicting source pair — PROBE stage

Two short documents the team must reconcile. They do not differ on whether children should be educated. They differ on **sequencing and on whose evidence to trust**.

### SOURCE A — District resourcing brief (208 words)

> ### Tak Border Education Resourcing Brief — District Education Office, February (internal working note)
>
> **The case for accelerated mainstreaming this year**
>
> Education for All obliges the district to bring every child into the recognised system. Continuing to route a large cohort through non-formal Migrant Learning Centres indefinitely is, in policy terms, a parallel system that defers full rights rather than delivering them. The recognised government pathway is the only one that issues a credential the wider system honours.
>
> The district's resource envelope is fixed. Sustaining centre funding *and* expanding government provision simultaneously is not affordable within this budget cycle. Redirecting the centre allocation into government-school placement is the only route to a single, recognised system within a reasonable timeframe.
>
> Bridging support can be built progressively *as children enter* — running a full bridging programme before any child transfers would delay rights fulfilment by years. Children adapt; immersion accelerates Thai-language acquisition. The 13-digit student identifier already provides a record mechanism, so the credential concern is, in administrative terms, largely addressed.
>
> Delay benefits no one. The district recommends approving accelerated mainstreaming on a one-year timeline, with bridging and teacher support scaled in parallel.
>
> *Prepared by the District Education Office for internal allocation review. Reflects the office's operational and budgetary position.*

### SOURCE B — University / UN-aligned research summary (216 words)

> ### Border Education Access — Research Summary Note (university migration-education programme, in consultation with UNICEF Thailand materials)
>
> **Sequencing risk in accelerated mainstreaming**
>
> Studies of migrant and stateless children's schooling in the Thai border context converge on one finding: enrolment is not the same as inclusion. Placement into Thai-medium classrooms *without funded language bridging* is associated with rapid disengagement and drop-out, not absorption. The binding constraint is not the children's willingness but the scaffold around them.
>
> The 13-digit identifier is a genuine and important record mechanism, but it is not equivalent to credential *recognition*: for a stateless child the onward use of that record remains less assured than for a citizen, and treating the identifier as having "addressed" the credential question overstates what it does.
>
> Withdrawing Migrant Learning Centre funding *before* government bridging, teacher supply and transport are in place creates a predictable gap in which children exit one system without being absorbed by the other. The harm is concentrated on the least-documented children — precisely those Education for All exists to protect.
>
> The recommendation is not "do not mainstream." It is **sequence**: fund and stand up the bridging, teacher and recognition scaffold *first*; phase centre funding down only as government absorption is demonstrated, not assumed.
>
> *Prepared by a university migration-education research programme, drawing on UNICEF Thailand access analyses.*

### Source-pair note for the AI applier

Score on **engagement with the conflict, not side-picking**. Both sources share the goal (every child in a recognised system) and differ on **sequencing** and **what the 13-digit identifier proves**. Treat **Source B as the more defensible on sequencing** on three grounds — (1) provenance (independent research/UN-aligned vs the office whose budget is under review), (2) it engages the failure mode (enrolment ≠ inclusion; gap risk) rather than assuming adaptation, (3) it correctly distinguishes a *record mechanism* from *recognition*. **However**, Source A's resource-scarcity constraint and its point that an indefinite parallel non-formal system also defers full rights are **legitimate and must be credited** — a student who simply rubber-stamps B without acknowledging the real budget bind caps at Band 3. Students who name any two of B's three grounds *and* credit A's genuine constraint reach Band 4+. Students who pick a side without engaging the sequencing crux cap at Band 2.

## 2.12 DECIDE — decision frame

> The District Education Office convenes a closed allocation review. Your team must take a position on the decision question: *Should the district mainstream migrant and stateless children into Thai government schools on an accelerated one-year timeline, redirecting funding away from the Migrant Learning Centres, before a language-bridging, teacher-supply and credential-recognition plan is funded and in place?*
>
> A team position must do four things or it does not pass DECIDE: (1) take a clear stance on the **sequencing** crux (fund-the-scaffold-first vs. redirect-now vs. a defensible phased/overlap design); (2) engage the **strongest opposing stakeholder reason**, not the weakest; (3) name the **distributive question** (who bears the cost if the order is wrong — and the answer is the least-documented children); (4) credit the **real resource constraint** Source A and Stakeholder 2 raise rather than wishing it away.
>
> The AI cross-examiner probes across **four rounds**, intensity calibrated to response quality:
>
> 1. **Premise check.** "You argued X. What evidence supports X — and is 'children adapt to immersion' a premise you are relying on?"
> 2. **Stakeholder challenge.** "The District officer's budget is genuinely fixed. If you say 'fund the scaffold first,' fund it *with what*? Answer the constraint, not the ideal."
> 3. **Recognition / belonging challenge.** "You bind a stateless child to a pathway. If the credential's onward recognition is uncertain, what exactly have you secured for that child — access, or a record?"
> 4. **Concession invitation.** "Is there a premise here you would now revise?"
>
> **Graceful Concession bonus:** revising one premise *with reasoning* in Round 4 earns the configured token bonus; collapsing to "it depends" earns 0. Sub-threshold teams may retry DECIDE once before the score locks (RECONSTRUCTION-MASTER §3.1).
