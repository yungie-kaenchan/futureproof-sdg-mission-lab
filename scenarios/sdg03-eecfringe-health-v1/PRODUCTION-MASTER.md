## 1.3 The empirical spine of the tension

This scenario is built on a **documented structural dynamic**, not an invented one. The spine, stated without fabricated figures:

- Thailand is an **aged society** moving toward super-aged status — a well-established national demographic fact.
- Large industrial-investment zones **draw working-age labour out of surrounding agrarian areas** into wage work and into the zone's towns — a standard, documented feature of regional industrialisation, of which the EEC is the contemporary eastern-Thailand instance.
- The combination produces **demographically hollowed fringe villages**: elders and grandchildren over-represented, the middle generation absent on shift work — a recognised "skipped-generation" / grandparent-headed-household pattern in rural Thailand.
- Primary care for these villages runs through a **tiered public system** (รพ.สต. → district hospital → provincial/regional) financed via the **NHSO universal-coverage scheme**, with the **อสม. volunteer network** as the household-level layer — all real architecture.
- The **policy gap is real, not fabricated**: concentrating scarce clinical staff where patient *volume* is highest (the EEC towns) is administratively efficient, but it under-serves low-volume, high-need hollowed villages — and the financing reform that would fund a distributed alternative is not yet in place. That tension between **allocative efficiency and health equity** is the genuine, unresolved dilemma.

## 1.5 Flagged uncertainties — use ranges, never invent

The following are knowingly **not** specified to a precise figure. Where they appear in student-facing text, the dossier uses qualifiers ("around," "in recent years," "reports describe," "more than"):

- ⚠ Exact share of working-age out-migration from any specific fringe sub-district — no single authoritative public figure; dossier says *"most working-age adults now spend the week in the EEC towns."*
- ⚠ Exact อสม.-to-household ratio in the fringe villages — held by the provincial health office / DoH; dossier uses *"each volunteer covers more households than the system was designed for."*
- ⚠ Exact distance/travel time from the fictional fringe village to its district hospital — fictionalised as **a journey of more than an hour each way by the available transport** for narrative tractability. Flagged here as a *narrative choice, not a verified figure.*
- ⚠ Exact provincial budget split between EEC-town facilities and fringe รพ.สต. — not publicly broken out; rendered as *"most of the new clinical posts went where the patient numbers were highest."*
- ⚠ Specific village, sub-district, district names — deliberately generic ("a fringe sub-district in the EEC hinterland").
- ⚠ Specific company, hospital, official, or scholar names — deliberately not used.
- ⚠ The "elder-care community fund" and "mobile-clinic + telehealth + strengthened-อสม. distributed model" are **plausible policy instruments**, presented as a *proposed* option in the decision, not as an existing programme.

## 1.6 Cultural-authenticity guard

Stakeholder voice authenticity (CLAUDE.md §11 + §15; RECONSTRUCTION §2 dignity guardrail):

- **The grandmother raising grandchildren** speaks with quiet competence and pride, not helplessness. She is the household's anchor, not its victim. Her concern is practical (getting to the district hospital, her blood-pressure refill, who watches the children if she is admitted), expressed plainly. **No pity narrative. No "poor old woman" framing.** She has agency and a clear ask.
- **End-of-life and mental-health content** is handled with restraint: named as real care needs of an ageing village (a neighbour's last months at home; the loneliness of elders whose children are away), never dramatised, never used for emotional manipulation. Dignity-first.
- **The provincial health officer** speaks in measured public-service English — responsible, constrained by budget and mandate, not callous. The efficiency position is *defensible from inside her situation*.
- **The EEC-town hospital administrator / health economist** speaks in clear analytical English — makes the genuine efficiency-and-volume case without being a caricature of a "bean counter."
- **The อสม. volunteer** speaks warmly, observationally, with concrete house-to-house detail — modelling how อสม. actually report: by household, by pattern, by name-they-won't-say.

**No stakeholder is positioned as "the wrong answer." All four positions are defensible from inside the speaker's situation.** The grandchildren and the absent middle generation are present in the scenario as people with reasons, not as a statistic.

# 2. SCENARIO TEXT

## 2.1 Title

**The Village the Boom Left Behind**
**หมู่บ้านที่ความเจริญทิ้งไว้**

## 2.2 Setting (28 words)

> An hour past the Eastern Economic Corridor's factory towns, fringe villages have thinned to elders and grandchildren — the working generation gone to the estates, the small district hospital far.

## 2.3 Core tension (22 words)

> Concentrating scarce health staff where the workers are is efficient; the hollowed villages they left behind grow old far from care.

## 2.4 Decision point (DECIDE stage)

> Should the provincial health office concentrate scarce clinical staff and budget in the higher-volume EEC-town facilities — efficient, where the patients are — or fund a costlier **distributed model** (mobile clinics, telehealth, strengthened อสม., an elder-care community fund) for the demographically hollowed fringe villages, **before** the larger health-financing reform that would pay for it is approved?

## 2.5 Ethical axes (controlled vocabulary)

1. **Economic development vs. health equity** — the growth that lifts the region's economy also pulls the carers out of the villages that most need care. Does the health system follow the money and the volume, or follow the need?
2. **Who bears the externalities of growth (rural ageing)** — the EEC's prosperity has a cost it does not pay: hollowed villages where elders age without nearby family or staff. Who carries that cost, and who decides?

### Part 1 — What the boom built, and what it left

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> In the east of Thailand, the government built a big industrial zone. It is called the Eastern Economic <vocab>corridor</vocab> — the EEC. It stretches across three provinces. It brought factories, jobs, and money.
>
> The work is in the EEC towns. So the young and middle-aged people went there. They work all week in the factories. They come back to the village only sometimes.
>
> Outside the busy towns are small villages. These villages used to be full of families. Now they are mostly old people and small children. The grandparents stay. The grandchildren stay with them. The parents are away at work. People call this a <vocab>hollowed</vocab> village. The middle is gone.
>
> The EEC is not bad. It gave the region jobs. But it also changed who lives in the villages — and that change is the start of this problem.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2 — baseline):**

> In eastern Thailand, the state developed a major industrial zone — the Eastern Economic <vocab>corridor</vocab>, or EEC — spanning three provinces. It brought factories, steady wages, and investment to the region.
>
> The work, however, is concentrated in the EEC's towns and estates. So the working-age generation followed it. Most now spend the week in the EEC towns and return to their home villages only on rest days, if at all. This is labour <vocab>migration</vocab> within a single region.
>
> Beyond the estate footprint lie the fringe villages. They were once full households. Today they are <vocab>demographic</vocab>ally <vocab>hollowed</vocab>: largely older adults and the grandchildren they raise, while the middle generation works away. Many homes are grandparent-headed.
>
> The EEC is not the villain of this case. It lifted the regional economy. But it also reshaped who actually lives in the fringe villages — and that reshaping is where this problem begins.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> Eastern Thailand hosts a flagship state industrial zone — the Eastern Economic <vocab>corridor</vocab> (EEC) — extending across three provinces and anchoring the region's manufacturing, petrochemical, and logistics economy. Its developmental case is real: investment, employment, and growth.
>
> That economic activity is, however, spatially concentrated in the EEC's towns and estates, and the working-age population has followed it. Most now reside in the EEC towns through the working week, returning to their home villages intermittently or not at all — intra-regional labour <vocab>migration</vocab> driven by a wage and reliability differential agriculture cannot match.
>
> Beyond the estate footprint, the fringe villages have undergone a pronounced <vocab>demographic</vocab> <vocab>hollowing</vocab>: older adults and the grandchildren in their care are over-represented, the intervening generation structurally absent. Grandparent-headed, "skipped-generation" households are common rather than exceptional.
>
> The EEC is not cast here as antagonist; its developmental contribution is conceded. The analytically salient point is that it has reconfigured the resident composition of the fringe villages — and that reconfiguration is the proximate origin of the dilemma that follows.

### Part 2 — Who is left, and what care they need

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> Think about who is now in a fringe village. Mostly old people. Many live alone, or with grandchildren. Their own children are away at work in the EEC towns.
>
> Old people need health care often. Many have <vocab>chronic</vocab> illness — illness that lasts a long time. Things like high blood pressure or diabetes need check-ups and medicine all the time. Some elders cannot move well. Some are sad and lonely because their family is far away. Some are near the end of life and want care at home.
>
> When you are old and your children are not there, small things become hard. Who takes you to the hospital? Who collects your medicine? Who notices if you are getting worse? In a full village, family did this. In a <vocab>hollowed</vocab> village, often no one is there to do it.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2 — baseline):**

> Consider who actually remains in a fringe village now: largely older adults, many living alone or with grandchildren, their adult children away on shift work in the EEC towns.
>
> Older populations carry a heavy and continuous care load. Many manage <vocab>chronic</vocab> conditions — hypertension, diabetes — that require regular monitoring and uninterrupted medication. Mobility declines with age. Isolation and low mood are common where family is absent for most of the week. Some residents are in their final months and wish to be cared for at home, with dignity.
>
> When an elder's children are not nearby, the ordinary supports collapse. Who accompanies them to the district hospital? Who collects a refill? Who notices early that a condition is worsening? In a full village, family absorbed this. In a <vocab>hollowed</vocab> village, frequently no one is left to.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> Consider the residual composition of a fringe village: predominantly older adults, a substantial proportion living alone or in grandparent-headed households, the adult children absent in EEC-town employment for most of the week.
>
> An aged population imposes a sustained, non-episodic care burden. <vocab>Chronic</vocab> non-communicable conditions — hypertension, diabetes — demand continuous surveillance and uninterrupted pharmacotherapy. Functional mobility attenuates with age; social isolation and depressive symptomatology are elevated where kin are absent for the working week; a minority are in end-of-life trajectories and express a preference for dignified care at home.
>
> Where an elder's adult children are not proximate, the informal supports that ordinarily mediate access to the formal system fail: accompaniment to the district hospital, prescription collection, early detection of clinical deterioration. In an intact village these were absorbed by family. In a <vocab>hollowed</vocab> village they frequently have no one to fall to.

### Part 3 — The system that is supposed to reach them

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> Thailand does have a health system for villages. There are three parts.
>
> First, the <vocab>volunteer (อสม.)</vocab> — village health volunteers, called อสม.. They are trained people from the village itself. They are not paid a real salary. > Second, a small village health centre (รพ.สต.) for everyday care.

> Third, a district hospital for bigger problems. It can be far — more than an hour away by the transport people have.

> The whole system is paid for through the country's health-coverage scheme, run by the National Health Security Office (NHSO). On paper, every village is covered. The problem is not the law. The problem is reach: the <vocab>primary care</vocab> network in the hollowed villages is thin, and the people in it are stretched.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2 — baseline):**

### Part 4 — The decision before you

**Tier 1 (A2–B1):**

> Now the problem is in front of the provincial health office. They do not have enough staff or money for everywhere. They must choose.
>
> One choice: put the staff and money in the EEC-town hospitals. That is where the most patients are — the workers. It is the most efficient choice. More people get served per baht.
>
> Other choice: spend more — and harder — to reach the hollowed villages. Send mobile clinics. Use phones and video for some visits (<vocab>telehealth</vocab>). Train and support more อสม.. Start a small fund for elder care at home. This costs more per person and serves fewer people. But the people it serves have the least access of all.
>
> And here is the hard part: the big change to health funding that would pay for the village option is **not approved yet**. So the office must decide *before* the money is sure.
>
> Your team will brief the provincial health board. The board will not accept "it depends." It needs a clear position — and reasons that hold up when the other side pushes back.

**Tier 2 (B1+–B2 — baseline):**

> The decision now sits with the provincial health office. Staff and budget are finite; they cannot be everywhere. A choice is forced.
>
> Option one: concentrate clinical staff and budget in the EEC-town facilities, where patient volume is highest because that is where the workers are. This is the efficient <vocab>allocation</vocab> — the most people served per baht spent.
>
> Option two: fund a costlier, harder distributed model for the hollowed villages — mobile clinics, <vocab>telehealth</vocab> for suitable visits, a strengthened and better-supported อสม. layer, and an elder-care community fund. It costs more per patient and reaches fewer patients — but those patients currently have the least access of anyone in the province.
>
> The hard edge: the larger health-financing reform that would pay for option two **is not yet approved**. The office must decide *before* the money is secured.
>
> Your team will brief the provincial health board. The board will not accept "it depends." It needs a defensible position — and reasoning that survives when other stakeholders push back.

**Tier 3 (B2+–C1):**

> The decision now devolves to the provincial health office. Personnel and budget are finite and non-ubiquitous; a choice is unavoidable.
>
> The first option concentrates clinical staffing and budget in the EEC-town facilities, where caseload density is greatest because the workforce is there. This is the allocatively efficient disposition — maximal population served per unit expenditure.
>
> The second funds a costlier, operationally harder distributed model for the hollowed villages: mobile clinics, <vocab>telehealth</vocab> for amenable encounters, a reinforced and better-resourced อสม. stratum, and an elder-care community fund. Its per-patient cost is higher and its reach narrower — yet its beneficiaries are precisely those with the lowest access in the province, a textbook <vocab>equity</vocab>-versus-efficiency tension.
>
> The decisive constraint: the broader health-financing reform that would underwrite option two **remains unapproved**. The office must commit *in advance* of secured financing — i.e., decide who bears the <vocab>externality</vocab> of growth before the instrument that would redistribute it exists.
>
> Your team will brief the provincial health board. The board will not entertain indeterminacy. It requires a defensible position and an evidentiary rationale robust to adversarial challenge from competing stakeholders.

>
> With finite staffing and a constrained provincial budget, the defensible allocation principle is cost-effectiveness: maximise health gain per baht across the whole population. The EEC-town facilities carry by far the highest patient throughput, because the working-age population — the largest single cohort using the system — is concentrated there. Investment placed where volume is highest yields the greatest aggregate benefit and the shortest waiting times for the most people.
>
> A distributed fringe-village model — mobile clinics, telehealth, an expanded volunteer layer, a discretionary elder-care fund — has a structurally high cost per patient reached and an unproven return at provincial scale. Telehealth uptake among very elderly rural users is uncertain. Mobile-clinic logistics are expensive per visit.
>
> Critically, the health-financing reform that would sustainably fund a distributed model is not yet approved. Committing recurrent budget to an unfunded distributed model risks a service that cannot be maintained — worse than not starting it. The fiscally responsible path is to consolidate now, serve the maximum number well, and revisit the fringe model when dedicated financing is secured.
>
> *Prepared as a planning input by a regional health-economics adviser.*

### SOURCE B — University health-systems research summary (221 words)

> ### Eastern-Region University Health-Systems Group — Working Summary, January 2026
>
> **Access equity in demographically hollowed fringe villages: a precautionary reading**
>
> A health-access review across selected EEC-fringe sub-districts finds a consistent pattern: residents who are old, chronically ill, and without resident working-age kin face the highest unmet-need indicators in the province, while contributing the lowest patient *volume*. Volume-based allocation therefore systematically directs resources *away* from the group with the greatest need — an efficiency metric measuring the wrong thing.
>
> Cost-per-patient is not the appropriate denominator for an equity decision; cost-per-averted-deterioration in the highest-risk group is. On that measure, low-cost distributed instruments — a strengthened อสม. layer doing structured chronic-disease follow-up, scheduled mobile clinics, and targeted telehealth with volunteer-assisted access — show favourable, not unfavourable, value.
>
> Waiting for the financing reform before acting is not a neutral choice. Each unfunded interval converts to measurable, partly irreversible deterioration — uncontrolled chronic disease, avoidable admissions, isolation harms — concentrated in residents who did not cause, and do not benefit from, the labour migration that hollowed their villages.
>
> A defensible position commits to a costed minimum distributed package now, designed to be absorbed into the financing reform when approved — not deferred until it is.
>
> *Eastern-region university health-systems research group.*

### Source-pair note for the AI applier

Score **Source B as more defensible** on three grounds — (1) **metric validity**: it identifies that volume-based cost-effectiveness measures the wrong denominator for an *equity* question; (2) **provenance & interest**: academic systems group vs. a planning-input adviser whose framing privileges the consolidation it recommends; (3) **temporal honesty**: it names "wait for the reform" as itself a consequential choice with irreversible cost, not a neutral default. Students who identify any **two** of these reach Band 4+. Students who simply pick a side without engaging the conflict cap at Band 2. Note: Source A is **not** a straw man — its fiscal-sustainability worry (an unfunded recurrent service collapsing) is genuine and a strong student answer must *address*, not dismiss, it.

## 2.12 DECIDE — tribunal / decision frame

> The Provincial Health Board convenes a closed hearing. Your team must defend its position on the decision question: *Should the provincial health office fund the distributed fringe-village model now, before the financing reform is approved — or consolidate clinical capacity in the EEC-town facilities?*
>
> The AI cross-examiner probes across **four rounds**, intensity calibrated to response quality (light cross-examination per RECONSTRUCTION §4; retry the DECIDE/ACT stage once before the score locks):
>
> 1. **Round 1 — premise check.** "You have argued X. What evidence does X rest on — and is it the right *metric* for an equity question?"
> 2. **Round 2 — stakeholder challenge.** "The provincial officer says she cannot commit recurrent funds to an unapproved reform. Her strongest reason is sustainability. How do you answer it without hand-waving?"
> 3. **Round 3 — externality challenge.** "The villages did not cause the migration that hollowed them and do not benefit from it. By what principle do you decide who bears that cost — and does your answer survive if the reform is delayed two more years?"
> 4. **Round 4 — concession invitation.** "Is there any premise in your position you would now revise?"
>
> **Graceful Concession bonus:** Teams that revise one premise *with reasoning* in Round 4 earn +10 Insight Tokens. Teams that collapse to "it depends" earn 0. A top answer does **not** pretend the efficiency case is empty — it shows a position that *carries* Source A's sustainability worry rather than ignoring it (e.g., a costed minimum package explicitly designed to fold into the reform).
>
> Output: a 200–300 word position statement that frames Panel 3 (THE INSIGHT) of the Final Task "Voice for Change" pitch.

## 3.2 Pass-threshold → SDG-3 Keystone

- The mission yields its **SDG-3 Keystone** when the learner **meets the bar**, not when they score perfectly (RECONSTRUCTION §3.1).
- **Bar:** all 5 stages completed (BRIEF · PROBE · DECIDE · ACT · DEBRIEF) **AND** composite mission score **≥ 60%** (configurable in this scenario's manifest).
- Sub-threshold learners may **retry the DECIDE/ACT stages once** before the score locks.
- The Keystone is **binary** (earned / not yet) — it is not graded and not spendable. It is the narrative key that, with the other five, unlocks the Final Task.
- **Teacher override** (instructor dashboard, logged with reason + timestamp) and the gated **`?demo=keystones`** bypass apply exactly as in RECONSTRUCTION §3.2 — the journey never traps a struggling learner, and judges can reach the Final Task in a 7-minute demo.

## 3.3 Longitudinal evidence-commitment → Final Task "Voice for Change"

The DEBRIEF (§2.13) is not a throwaway exit slip. The learner's stated **position** and the **named trade-off it gives up** are persisted and carried into the Final Task "Voice for Change" (RECONSTRUCTION §8):

- The SDG-3 mission contributes the **health-equity strand** of the capstone — the learner must stand behind, in front of an authentic audience, the position they committed to here.
- Continuity is assessed: a Final Task position that *contradicts* the SDG-3 DEBRIEF commitment without acknowledging the change is flagged for the teacher as an un-owned reversal (good learners may change their mind — but must say so and say why; this mirrors the DECIDE Round-4 Graceful Concession logic, applied longitudinally).
- This makes the mission's evaluative spine **traceable end-to-end**: BRIEF facts → PROBE source-conflict reasoning → DECIDE defended position → ACT register-appropriate artifact → DEBRIEF owned trade-off → Final Task authentic-audience defence. Each stage's evidence feeds the next; nothing is assessed in isolation.
